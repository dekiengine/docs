Screens

One project runs on any screen. Every device renders at its own native resolution, and the camera decides how much world that shows.

The camera

Setting What it does
Ortho Height How much world the camera shows top to bottom, in meters. The width follows the screen's shape. In Pixels mode the inspector shows it at your pixels per meter: 11.25 m at 16 px/m reads 180 px.
Pixel Perfect Scales by whole numbers only and snaps the camera and everything drawn to the art-pixel grid. For pixel art.
Projection Orthographic (flat, for 2D) or Perspective (a vertical Field of View, for 3D meshes).

A bigger screen of the same shape shows the same world, drawn bigger. A wider screen shows more at the sides; a narrower one shows less. To get closer, make the ortho height smaller.

Pixels per meter (Project Settings > Units) is how dense your art is: a 16 px sprite at 16 px/m is one meter wide.

What each screen sees

A camera with an ortho height of 11.25 m:

Screen What it shows
1280x720 (16:9) 20 x 11.25 m
1920x1080 (16:9) 20 x 11.25 m, drawn 1.5x
320x240 (4:3) 15 x 11.25 m: the sides of the 16:9 view are cropped
2560x1080 (21:9) 26.7 x 11.25 m: more at the sides
240x320 (portrait) 8.4 x 11.25 m

Keep what matters inside the narrowest screen you target, and build a little world beyond it for wider ones, or the player sees the camera's clear color there.

Pinning to the screen

A HUD belongs to the screen, not to the world: hearts in the top-left corner, a score in the top-right. Add Screen Anchor to the object and pick the point in its grid: a corner, the middle of an edge, or the centre. Its Offset is the distance from that point in meters, with Y up, so hearts 0.875 m in from the left and 1 m down are Top Left with an offset of (0.875, -1).

The object then stays at that point on every screen and while the camera moves. It is sized in meters like everything else, so it scales with the camera: the same size on a 320x240 and a 536x240 screen, three times the pixels at 720p.

  • The Transform's position is set by the anchor and shows greyed out. Rotation and scale still come from the Transform, and children follow the anchored object as usual.
  • In the Scene view the object sits where it would on the screen the Play view shows; switching that screen moves it. Dragging it changes its offset. A line joins the selected object to its point.
  • The scene file stores the offset, so it does not change with the screen.

Recipes

2D pixel art. Set the ortho height to your art's native height: 15 m for 240 px at 16 px/m, for example. Turn on Pixel Perfect. A 640x480 screen draws every art pixel as 2x2, 1280x720 as 3x3, 400x300 at 1x with a little more world. Pixel Perfect rounds down, so the camera shows a little more than its ortho height rather than cutting any of it; a screen too small for 1x crops.

2D hi-res or vector art. Set the ortho height to what your main screen should show, and pixels per meter to your art's density. Every screen scales smoothly. The renderer samples nearest-neighbour, so art shrunk a long way (1280 to 320) shimmers when it moves: author near your smallest screen.

2D with 3D meshes. As for 2D, with the camera's projection set to Perspective. The field of view is vertical like the ortho height, so the meshes and the sprites line up on every screen. Sprites still draw flat; placing them in 3D comes next.

3D. Set the camera to Perspective and its field of view. Like the ortho height it is vertical: a wider screen sees more at the sides.

Previewing

The Play view's toolbar picks the screen to look at, as Unity's Game view does:

  • Free Aspect: the panel's own size.
  • An aspect ratio (16:9, 16:10, 4:3, 3:2, 21:9, 9:16): the largest screen of that shape that fits the panel.
  • A resolution: any platform's screen, or a custom width and height.

Next to it is the screen's pixel format. Play runs at that size and format. The size can change while playing, as if the device's screen changed; the format is fixed until Stop. The camera's gizmo in the scene view outlines what that screen sees; with no camera, the scene view draws the screen around the origin. The Play view remembers the choice per project.

DekiEditor --render-scenes renders at the active platform's screen in RGB565; DEKI_RENDER_SIZE=WxH renders another size and DEKI_RENDER_FORMAT another format. --export --platform <id> exports for a target other than the active one.

Colour formats

Every screen also has its own pixel format: RGB565 on most small panels, full colour on a desktop. Two things follow it.

  • The game draws in the screen's format. The display says what it takes when it starts, like its size. There is no project-wide colour format.
  • Images are stored for the screen they will be shown on. An image's Format (the texture inspector) is Automatic by default: RGB565 on an RGB565 target, or RGB565A8 when the image has transparency; RGB888, or RGBA8888 with transparency, on a full-colour target. The texture inspector's Targets section sets it, as a stack of cards: Default first, with the Format (and Max Size) every target uses, then one per target, headed by its name and the memory the image takes there. A folded card says what it stores; open, it shows its values (a target's are dimmed when they come from Default). Pick a value in a card to override it; a dot marks the override, and Default (...) at the top of the list goes back. The export stores each image as the target it is for wants it.
  • Max Size, next to the Format, works like Unity's: an image whose larger side is over it is stored scaled so that side is exactly Max Size, e.g. a big background kept small on one board. None (the default) never shrinks. A shrunk sprite keeps its size in the world and its frames: they stay in the image's pixels, and each is resampled on its own so neighbours do not bleed. Chroma-keyed images are shrunk by picking pixels rather than blending them, so the key colour stays exact. The editor shows the active target's size too; the Spritesheet editor always works on the original. Sidecar: settings.texture.maxSize, and per target settings.texture.targets.<id>: {"format", "maxSize"} (0 = None there).

A platform's colorFormat is the format of its screen. The editor's own cache follows the active platform, so what the scene view shows is what that target gets. The Play view's format dropdown previews another; images are converted as they are drawn, so any format draws on any screen.

3D models take the same Format and Max Size settings in their .data file, and draw into every framebuffer format. A model's texture is stored at powers of two, so Max Size caps them; with None it is capped at 256, the size a small board can hold resident.

On the device

The framebuffer is the display's own size and format, read when the display starts. One build runs on screens of different sizes. A platform's screenWidth, screenHeight and colorFormat are what the editor previews and what the desktop simulator opens its window as.
